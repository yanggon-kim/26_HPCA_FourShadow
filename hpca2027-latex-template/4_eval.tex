\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Scaling Across the SIMT Hierarchy}\label{sec:eval_hierarchy}

The last parts of Sections~\ref{sec:eval_threads} and~\ref{sec:eval_iw} answer Q2 (Section~\ref{sec:mot_point}) at the design points around NVIDIA's.

\subsubsection{Threads/Warp}\label{sec:eval_threads}
Fig.~\ref{fig:threads_sweep}(a) compares TCU SGEMM with scalar FP32 execution
at the same thread count. Packing more low-precision elements raises
speedup from 3.55--6.44$\times$ for TF32 to 9.53--27.46$\times$ for FP8.
Most notably, we find that the 16-thread configuration is consistently the strongest and reaches
27.46$\times$, while increasing to 32 threads (the established default warp-size in all modern GPU architectures) does not improve performance
further. This shows that Tensor Core scaling is non-monotonic with warp width
and depends significantly on tile geometry and pipeline utilization.

The 16-thread advantage of Fig.~\ref{fig:threads_sweep}(a) carries over to warp-group MMA, and the design points around NVIDIA's show why. A FourShadow warp-group step costs a fixed micro-op sequence, set by the accumulator count and sparsity, plus about one cycle per 64-byte operand line. From 8 to 16 threads every micro-op covers four times the work at the same fixed cost; from 16 to 32 threads only $M$ doubles, which adds $A$-operand lines in SS mode. Per lane at (IW-4, NW-4), 16 threads per warp is therefore 7--15\% ahead of 32 in SS mode (2.11 against 1.98 MAC per cycle per thread at $\mathit{NRC}{=}32$, Fig.~\ref{fig:q2_nt_iw}(a)) and level with it in RS mode.

\answerbox{Answer \#1 for Q2}{Tensor-core throughput per lane peaks at 16 threads per warp for warp-group MMA, because a micro-op's fixed cost is spread over four times the work from 8 to 16 threads, while from 16 to 32 threads the added work brings added operand lines; NVIDIA's 32-thread point sits on this plateau: 16 threads ties with it in RS mode and leads by 7--15\% in SS mode.}

\subsubsection{Warps/Warpgroup (Issue Width)}\label{sec:eval_iw}
Fig.~\ref{fig:iw_sweep}(b) normalizes each configuration against an issue
width of one. An issue width of two provides 1.21--1.48$\times$ speedup,
while four reaches up to 1.84$\times$. Increasing to eight only peaks at 2.02$\times$, with the 8- and 16-thread designs largely saturating after
four and the 32-thread design even regresses. This proves that wider issue provides diminishing returns once operand delivery and
non-TCU execution become the dominant costs.

At the instruction level, warp-group MMA on FourShadow shows where these returns go. Fig.~\ref{fig:q2_nt_iw}(b) streams back-to-back warp-group MMAs at 32 threads per warp and issue widths of 1, 2 and 4, a narrower range than the whole-kernel sweep of Fig.~\ref{fig:iw_sweep}(b), which extends to 8. Each doubling of the issue width raises throughput by $\times$1.71 and then $\times$1.58 in RS mode at $\mathit{NRC}{=}16$, and by $\times$1.55 and then $\times$1.38 in SS mode at $\mathit{NRC}{=}32$. The sparse per-$K$ speedup falls as the issue width grows, from 1.275$\times$ to 1.171$\times$ in RS mode and from 1.349$\times$ to 1.228$\times$ in SS mode. Sparsity shortens the fixed micro-op part of a step, from 72 to 53 cycles in RS mode at $\mathit{NRC}{=}16$, while the operand lines, which sparsity barely reduces, grow with the number of tensor blocks.

\answerbox{Answer \#2 for Q2}{A wider warpgroup gives diminishing gains and a smaller sparse speedup, because the operand lines of a step grow with the number of tensor blocks while sparsity shortens only the fixed micro-op part: from an issue width of 1 to 4 at 32 threads per warp, the sparse speedup falls from 1.349$\times$ to 1.228$\times$ in SS mode.}

\subsection{Warp-Group Tensor Core Design Space Exploration}\label{sec:eval_wgmma}

The first three parts explore FourShadow's warp-group design space; Section~\ref{sec:eval_wg_delivery} answers Q1 (Section~\ref{sec:mot_causes}) by comparing it with an NVIDIA H100 (configuration in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\subsubsection{Number of Accumulator Registers (NRC) Scaling}\label{sec:eval_nrc}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:nrc_sweep}, at (IW-2, NW-4) and 32 threads per warp, increasing NRC from 8 to 16 reduces
cycles by 30--32\% in both RS and SS modes, and extending SS to NRC${}=32$
reduces cycles by approximately 49\% over NRC${}=8$; at (IW-4, NW-4) the two reductions are 39.7--40.0\% and 59.6--60.1\%. One H100 SM computing the same GEMM saves 19--50\% from $N{=}16$ to 32 and 41--65\% from 16 to 64, which brackets the FourShadow values. In these GEMM kernels SS is only 1--4\%
faster than RS at the same NRC, a property of how the kernels stage the operands, since per instruction an SS step costs 1.26--1.43$\times$ an RS step at (IW-4, NW-4) (Section~\ref{sec:eval_wg_delivery}); its main advantage is mostly enabling the
largest accumulator tile size possible. However, the gains remain sublinear because memory,
control, and outer $K$-loop work do not shrink with accumulator capacity.

\subsubsection{TCU SRAM vs.\ Register File WGMMA Descriptor Load}
Moving the WGMMA descriptors into TCU SRAM increases cycles by
1.2--6.5\% relative to register-file delivery. The penalty is largest for short SS kernels
and decreases as more computation amortizes descriptor setup. Relieving
the pressure of two register operands does not offset the added
shared-memory store, synchronization, and AGU traffic. Small control
metadata should therefore remain in the register file unless those
registers enable a larger accumulator configuration or there is extreme register pressure.

On the H100, a warp-group MMA that takes $A$ from shared memory is limited by operand delivery: every SS issue interval of a $64{\times}N{\times}16$ instruction, dense and sparse, equals the larger of the math time, $N/2$ cycles, and the operand bytes divided by 128 bytes per cycle, so dense SS matches RS only at $N{\geq}64$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:h100_ss_delivery}(a)). Our H100 runs reproduce all 24 published values of the H800 study~\cite{luo2025dissectingnvidiahopperarchitecture}, and the rule accounts for them, including the 128 and 144 cycles of sparse RS and SS at $N{=}256$ cited in Section~\ref{sec:mot_causes}. FourShadow shows the same SS penalty (Fig.~\ref{fig:h100_ss_delivery}(b): 1.43$\times$ and 1.26$\times$ at $\mathit{NRC}$ 8 and 16, against 1.54$\times$ and 1.50$\times$ on the H100), and its cause is visible: one cycle per 64-byte tile-buffer line. The two differ in how the costs combine. FourShadow adds the line cycles to the math, so SS stays behind RS at every $\mathit{NRC}$ where both run; the H100 takes the larger of the two, which implies that it fetches the next operands while the current math runs.

\answerbox{Answer \#1 for Q1}{A warp-group MMA with operands in shared memory is bounded by delivering them at 128 bytes per cycle, and the H100 overlaps that delivery with the math where FourShadow adds it to the math, so on the H100 SS matches RS once the tile is wide enough to hide the delivery ($N{\geq}64$).}

\subsection{Sparse Tensor Core Design Space Exploration}\label{sec:eval_sparse}

This subsection answers Q1, the internal mechanisms behind tensor-core sparsity (Section~\ref{sec:mot_causes}). The first two parts explore the sparse design space of FourShadow: the symmetric and asymmetric layouts of warp-level MMA, and the two operand-staging modes of warp-group MMA. The second part and the last two give the answers by comparing FourShadow with NVIDIA GPUs: warp-group MMA with an H100, and warp-level MMA with 2:4 sparsity with an RTX 5080 and an H100 (Sections~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_wg}, \ref{sec:eval_sparse_halves} and~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_meta}, configuration in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\subsubsection{Symmetric vs.\ Asymmetric Sparse Configurations}
Sparsity speeds up the asymmetric layouts in every cell of
Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_sym_sweep} and the symmetric layouts less. The 8- and
32-thread cells span 1.24--1.78$\times$, with the maximum for TF32 at
$K{=}1024$ and 8 threads, and both symmetric cells sit below both
asymmetric cells in 11 of 12 rows (the exception: TF32 at $K{=}256$,
1.45$\times$ at 16 threads against 1.37$\times$ at 8). At 4 threads
sparsity loses in nine of 12 cells (0.71--0.83$\times$); at 16 threads
it gains modestly, 0.91--1.45$\times$ and above 1 in 11 of 12 cells. The
ordering follows Section~\ref{sec:design_sparse}: only the asymmetric
layouts halve the micro-ops of a sparse WMMA. The symmetric layouts keep
the dense micro-op count, which therefore explains neither the 4-thread
loss nor the 16-thread gain; their source is not isolated.

The pinned dense and sparse warp-group GEMM kernels reproduce the ratio of Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_wg_sweep}, 2.22--2.35$\times$ in whole-kernel cycles for $K$ from 128 to 1024, at (IW-4, NW-4), 8 threads per warp and $\mathit{NRC}{=}8$. The ratio exceeds 2$\times$ because the two kernels do unequal work per $K$ step: the warp-group MMA is 2.2--2.7\% of the dense loop step, and the rest is the staging of the $A$ and $B$ tiles, which differs between the kernels in element count and index arithmetic. With the dense $A$ staged in the sparse kernel's layout, sparsity gains 1.60--1.72$\times$ in RS mode and 1.55--1.66$\times$ in SS mode; the $A$ copy and the $B$ indexing still differ. On the H100, with equal dense and sparse staging, the $128{\times}128{\times}K$ GEMM on one SM gains 1.00--1.55$\times$.

The H100 warp-group instruction itself halves its per-$K$ cost with $A$ in registers and a wide tile: for one CTA and a $64{\times}64$ tile, sparse warp-group MMA gains 2.00$\times$ with $A$ in registers and 1.33$\times$ with $A$ in shared memory, and no warp-group speedup on the H100 exceeds 2.00$\times$. A sparse instruction covers twice the $K$ of a dense one with the same $A$ bytes and twice the $B$ bytes, so per $K$ it halves the math and the $A$ bytes but not the $B$ bytes. With $A$ in registers and a wide tile, the $B$ delivery fits under the halved math (Section~\ref{sec:eval_wg_delivery}); with $A$ in shared memory the 2\,KB compressed $A$ is added to every instruction's delivery, which then sets the time.

\answerbox{Answer \#2 for Q1}{2:4 sparsity halves a warp-group MMA's per-$K$ math and $A$ bytes but not its $B$ bytes, so the instruction reaches the nominal 2$\times$ only when $A$ sits in registers and the tile is wide enough for the $B$ delivery to fit under the halved math: on the H100, 2.00$\times$ at $N{=}64$ with $A$ in registers against 1.33$\times$ with $A$ in shared memory.}

\subsubsection{Only Part of the Sparse Cost Halves}\label{sec:eval_sparse_halves}

For the $64{\times}64{\times}K$ FP16 GEMM of Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(a), the dense-over-sparse speedup rises from 1.16$\times$ at $K{=}32$ to 1.50$\times$ at $K{=}512$ on the RTX 5080, and from 1.07$\times$ to 1.56$\times$ on FourShadow at 32 threads per warp. Sparsity is faster in each of the 17 shapes measured on both platforms, and the two speedups differ by a median of 6.2\% at 32 threads and 9.0\% at 8 threads; at $128{\times}128{\times}1024$ they diverge, which we leave unexplained (1.40$\times$ on the RTX 5080 against 1.51$\times$ and 1.65$\times$ on FourShadow at 8 and 32 threads). Over issue widths of 1, 2 and 4 with 4 or 16 warps, at 8 and 32 threads, FourShadow stays below 2$\times$: its maximum is 1.92$\times$, for the INT8 $128{\times}128{\times}1024$ GEMM at (IW-1, NW-16) and 32 threads.

We fit the cycles of each $K$ sweep as $C_{\mathrm{dense}} = c + (a+d)K$ and $C_{\mathrm{sparse}} = c' + (a/2+d)K$, where $a$ is the per-$K$ cost that 2:4 sparsity halves and $d$ the per-$K$ cost that it leaves unchanged ($c$ and $c'$ do not depend on $K$); as $K$ grows the speedup approaches $1/(1-f/2)$, where $f = a/(a+d)$ is the halvable share. For FP16, $f$ is 69.7\% on the RTX 5080 and 78.5\% on FourShadow at 32 threads, which gives the dashed lines of Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(a) at 1.53$\times$ and 1.65$\times$. Here $a$ contains the tensor-core math and the $A$ load, which halve together, and $d$ the $B$ load and the metadata.

Only the FP16 fits are valid on the RTX 5080: the TF32 fit has a negative dense intercept, $-$50,243 cycles, and the INT8 fit has only four values of $K$, so Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(b) shows FP16 alone. On an H100 the same $64{\times}64{\times}K$ FP16 GEMM gives a halvable share of 54.4\% and a bound of 1.37$\times$, and FourShadow at 8 threads per warp gives 50.7\% and 1.34$\times$. The share therefore differs between GPU generations and between FourShadow configurations, while the cost structure, a per-$K$ cost of which sparsity halves only a part, holds on all of them. For warp-group MMA on the H100 the data type does not matter: TF32, FP16, BF16 and E4M3 give the same issue intervals and latencies within 0.042 cycles.

\answerbox{Answer \#3 for Q1}{2:4 sparsity halves only part of the per-$K$ cost of a warp-level tensor-core GEMM, so its speedup stays below 2$\times$ and depends on the size of that halvable part: for FP16 it is 69.7\% on the RTX 5080 (bound 1.53$\times$) and 54.4\% on the H100 (1.37$\times$).}

\subsubsection{One NVIDIA Warp Hides Its Metadata Load; FourShadow Needs Several}\label{sec:eval_sparse_meta}
On the RTX 5080, one CTA of one warp reloads the metadata, an ordinary register operand of \texttt{mma.sp}, in each of 256 iterations of a sparse FP16 MMA, before (early) or after (late) 0 to 64 filler operations (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(c)). The late reload adds 363.1 to 364.2 cycles per iteration at every filler distance; the early reload adds 326.2 cycles with no filler and 0.3 cycles with 64 filler operations. An early load thus costs the maximum of the load and the filler, and a late load their sum: the two rules, without a fitted constant, reproduce the cycles within 0.13\% for FP16 and within 0.72\% over six data formats. With 16 filler operations the profiled long-scoreboard stall share is 26.8\% early and 55.2\% late, at the same 11,858 instructions and 269 load requests.

An H100 follows the same two rules. One warp's late reload adds 293.9 to 295.1 cycles per iteration, and its early reload falls from 246.1 cycles with no filler to 2.1 cycles with 64 filler operations; the rules reproduce these cycles within 0.45\%. One warpgroup issuing sparse warp-group MMA behaves alike: its late reload adds 233 to 287 cycles, and its early reload 189 to 239 cycles with no filler and $-$3 to 3 cycles with 64 filler operations, with the maximum rule within 1.9\% and the sum rule within 0.14\%.

On FourShadow at (IW-1, NW-4), a dedicated instruction loads the metadata into a per-warp memory (Section~\ref{sec:design_sparse}), and with one CTA of one active warp the reload adds 9.0 cycles per iteration, early or late, at 8 and 32 threads per warp. When four one-warp CTAs share the issue lane at 32 threads per warp, the reload adds 59.3 cycles per CTA with no filler, and from 4 to 64 filler operations the late reload stays between 58.0 and 62.1 cycles while the early reload falls from 28.7 to 7.6 cycles (sum and maximum rules within 1.7\% and 4.6\%). This separation is stated for 32 threads only, because at 8 threads the late reload also falls, from 51.9 to 11.6 cycles; the scope is a simulation of one-warp CTAs on a lane of four warp slots, and absolute cycles are not compared across the platforms.

\answerbox{Answer \#4 for Q1}{The cost of sparse metadata depends on where the kernel issues the load and on what can run while it is pending: one warp of the RTX 5080 or the H100, and one warpgroup of the H100, hides an early load behind its own work, whereas FourShadow at (IW-1, NW-4) and 32 threads per warp hides it only behind other warps sharing its issue lane.}

\subsubsection{ASIC Synthesis and Timing}

\subsubsection{Threads/Warp Area--Performance Tradeoff}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} combines synthesized TCU area with end-to-end application evaluation performance. Llama~2 prefill reaches 12.7$\times$ at $NT{=}16$
and then slightly regresses at $NT{=}32$, despite the TCU area more
than doubling. Decode shows a stronger optimum, rising to
6.2$\times$ at $NT{=}16$ before falling to approximately 3$\times$
at $NT{=}32$. NeRF instead continues to benefit from wider warps and
reaches its highest speedup at $NT{=}32$. The preferred warp width is
therefore workload-dependent, although $NT{=}16$ consistently provides the best
area--performance ratio for both Llama~2 phases.

\subsubsection{Issue-Width Area--Performance Tradeoff}
Fig.~\ref{fig:iw_area_scaling} shows that TCU area scales almost
linearly with issue width, increasing from 58.6\,k$\mu$m$^2$ at
$IW{=}1$ to 115.6 and 230.6\,k$\mu$m$^2$ at $IW{=}2$ and $IW{=}4$.
However, performance scales much more slowly, reaching 1.37--1.41$\times$ at
$IW{=}2$ and 1.66--1.70$\times$ at $IW{=}4$. Doubling the area again
to an estimated 455--462\,k$\mu$m$^2$ at $IW{=}8$ raises speedup to
only 1.70--1.81$\times$. Thus, $IW{=}4$ forms the practical optimal on the curve, beyond which additional TCU blocks and larger warp-groups are increasingly
limited by operand delivery and non-TCU execution rather than
arithmetic capacity.
