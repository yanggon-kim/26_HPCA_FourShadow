\subsection{Scaling Across the SIMT Hierarchy}\label{sec:eval_hierarchy}

The last parts of Sections~\ref{sec:eval_threads} and~\ref{sec:eval_iw} answer Q2 (Section~\ref{sec:intro}) at the design points around NVIDIA's.

\subsubsection{Threads/Warp}\label{sec:eval_threads}
Fig.~\ref{fig:threads_sweep}(a) compares TCU SGEMM with scalar FP32 execution
at the same thread count. Packing more low-precision elements raises
speedup from 3.55--6.44$\times$ for TF32 to 9.53--27.46$\times$ for FP8.
Most notably, we find that the 16-thread configuration is consistently the strongest and reaches
27.46$\times$, while increasing to 32 threads (the established default warp-size in all modern GPU architectures) does not improve performance
further. This shows that Tensor Core scaling is non-monotonic with warp width
and depends significantly on tile geometry and pipeline utilization.

Warp-group MMA keeps this advantage, as the Block equations of Fig.~\ref{fig:tiling_equations} show. Eqs.~(\ref{eq:block_cap})--(\ref{eq:block_tc}) arrange the $\mathit{block\_cap}{=}\mathit{NT}$ FEDP units of a micro-op as a near-square $\mathit{tcM}{\times}\mathit{tcN}$ grid, and each lane's dot product is $\mathit{tcK}{=}\mathit{block\_cap}/\max(\mathit{tcM},\mathit{tcN})$ words: the block is $4{\times}2{\times}2$, $4{\times}4{\times}4$ and $8{\times}4{\times}4$ at 8, 16 and 32 threads, or 4, 8 and 8 FP16 multiply-accumulates (MACs) per lane and micro-op. A warp issues $2\,\mathit{NRC}$ micro-ops per dense step at every $\mathit{NT}$, each at a fixed cost, plus about one cycle per 64-byte operand line. A lane's work thus doubles from 8 to 16 threads but not from 16 to 32, where only $\mathit{tcM}$ doubles, and with it $A$: 16 to 32 shared-memory lines per SS step ($\mathit{IW}{=}4$, $\mathit{NRC}{=}32$). In MACs per cycle over the warpgroup's $\mathit{IW}{\times}\mathit{NT}$ threads, at (IW-4, NW-4), 16 threads is 11--19\% ahead of 32 in SS mode (3.48 against 3.14 at $\mathit{NRC}{=}32$, Fig.~\ref{fig:q2_nt_iw}(a)) and level in RS mode.

\answerbox{Answer \#1 for Q2}{Per-lane warp-group MMA throughput peaks at 16 threads per warp, because a lane's dot product doubles from 8 to 16 threads but not from 16 to 32; NVIDIA's 32-thread point sits on this plateau (level in RS mode, 11--19\% behind in SS mode).}

\subsubsection{Warps/Warpgroup (Issue Width)}\label{sec:eval_iw}
Fig.~\ref{fig:iw_sweep}(b) normalizes each configuration against an issue
width of one. An issue width of two provides 1.21--1.48$\times$ speedup,
while four reaches up to 1.84$\times$. Increasing to eight only peaks at 2.02$\times$, with the 8- and 16-thread designs largely saturating after
four. The returns diminish because instruction issue, not the tensor core or shared memory, bounds the kernel. In the dense warp-group GEMM loop at (IW-4, NW-4), 8 threads per warp and $\mathit{NRC}{=}8$, the warp-group MMA is 2.1--2.5\% of a $K$ step, and the rest stages $A$ and $B$ into shared memory on the integer and load/store units. With four warps per core, the kernel issues 0.42--0.44 instructions per cycle at every issue width and uses 1.5--1.7\% of the shared-memory bank bandwidth, so a wider issue gains only the instructions saved by staging $B$ once for a larger warpgroup. Growing the warps with the issue width, to (IW-2, NW-8), (IW-4, NW-16) and (IW-8, NW-32), speeds up a $K$ step over (IW-1, NW-4) by 2.4--2.5$\times$, 3.2--3.5$\times$ and 3.6--3.9$\times$ rather than 2, 4 and 8$\times$: from (IW-4, NW-16) on, the core schedules a warp in 93--99\% of cycles, because its front end fetches one warp instruction per cycle whatever the issue width. The warp-group MMA escapes this bound because each instruction expands into $2\,\mathit{NRC}$ micro-ops that keep its warp's tensor block busy.

At the instruction level, the warp-group MMA of FourShadow scales nearly linearly with the issue width. Fig.~\ref{fig:q2_nt_iw}(b) streams back-to-back warp-group MMAs at 32 threads per warp and issue widths of 1, 2 and 4, a narrower range than the whole-kernel sweep of Fig.~\ref{fig:iw_sweep}(b), which extends to 8. Each doubling of the issue width raises throughput by $\times$2.00 and again by $\times$2.00 in RS mode at $\mathit{NRC}{=}16$, and by $\times$1.89 and then $\times$1.80 in SS mode at $\mathit{NRC}{=}32$. As the issue width grows, the sparse per-$K$ speedup stays at 1.275$\times$ in RS mode and rises from 1.349$\times$ to 1.417$\times$ in SS mode. Sparsity shortens the fixed micro-op part of a step, from 72 to 53 cycles in RS mode at $\mathit{NRC}{=}16$. The issue-width results are measured on FourShadow alone; a commercial GPU fixes its warpgroup at four warps.

\answerbox{Answer \#2 for Q2}{A wider warpgroup scales the warp-group MMA but not the kernel around it: the core fetches one warp instruction per cycle, so the kernel's other instructions issue at most about one per cycle, while each warp-group MMA expands into $2\,\mathit{NRC}$ micro-ops that keep its tensor block busy; with the warps grown to match, an issue width of 8 speeds up a $K$ step of the GEMM by 3.6--3.9$\times$, not 8$\times$.}


\subsubsection{Roofline Model Performance Analysis}\label{sec:eval_roofline}
Fig.~\ref{fig:roofline} shows that WMMA raises arithmetic intensity from
0.25 to 3.61 FLOP/byte and throughput from 2.65 to 23.21 FLOP/cycle.
Sparse WMMA further reaches 33.49 FLOP/cycle. On the other hand, while WGMMA has the highest achieved operational intensities at 5.82 and 7.11 FLOP/byte, it only reaches 2.31 and
5.85 FLOP/cycle for dense and sparse execution respectively. This indicates that Tensor Core designs, especially with sparsity enabled are able to move fairly memory-bound kernels into the compute bound region. In the simulated kernels the shared-memory reads of an SS step amount to one $B$ tile per warpgroup, 1.00 per step, as in the RTL design (Section~\ref{sec:design_wgmma}).

\subsubsection{Number of Accumulator Registers (NRC) Scaling}\label{sec:eval_nrc}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:nrc_sweep}, at (IW-2, NW-4) and 32 threads per warp, increasing NRC from 8 to 16 reduces
cycles by 33--34\% in both RS and SS modes, and extending SS to NRC${}=32$
reduces cycles by approximately 50\% over NRC${}=8$; at (IW-4, NW-4) the two reductions are 40.0--40.3\% and 60.1--60.6\%. One H100 SM computing the same GEMM saves 19--50\% from $N{=}16$ to 32 and 41--65\% from 16 to 64, which brackets the FourShadow values. In these GEMM kernels SS is only 1--4\%
faster than RS at the same NRC, a property of how the kernels stage the operands, since per instruction an SS step costs 1.32--1.45$\times$ an RS step at (IW-4, NW-4); its main advantage is mostly enabling the
largest accumulator tile size possible. However, the gains remain sublinear because memory,
control, and outer $K$-loop work do not shrink with accumulator capacity.

\subsubsection{TCU SRAM vs.\ Register File WGMMA Descriptor Load}\label{sec:eval_desc}
Moving the WGMMA descriptors into TCU SRAM increases cycles by
1.2--6.5\% relative to register-file delivery. The penalty is largest for short SS kernels
and decreases as more computation amortizes descriptor setup. Relieving
the pressure of two register operands does not offset the added
shared-memory store, synchronization, and AGU traffic. Small control
metadata should therefore remain in the register file unless those
registers enable a larger accumulator configuration or there is extreme register pressure.

\subsubsection{Reading \texorpdfstring{$A$}{A} from Shared Memory Delays Every Warp-Group MMA}\label{sec:eval_delay_a}
% TODO(delay-a-rtl): the FourShadow delay values come from SimX, which fetches the whole A tile in 64-B lines; III-B describes the RTL A buffer (one K stripe, one bank row per request). See 00_doc/vortex-wgdxa2k/results/DELAY_A.md.
In RS mode the compute micro-ops read $A$ from registers \texttt{f24}--\texttt{f27}, and only $B$ crosses the shared-memory port. In SS mode each tensor block must fill its $A$ buffer through that port, which all $\mathit{IW}{+}1$ tile buffers share (Section~\ref{sec:design_wgmma}), so its first multiplication waits for those reads. On FourShadow this wait adds 28--31 cycles to a dense warp-group MMA (bars in Fig.~\ref{fig:delay_a}(a)). It does not grow with $N$: $A$ is the $64{\times}16$ tile of every instruction, while $N$ widens only $B$ and the micro-ops, which both modes pay. It follows the bytes of $A$: the compressed 1\,KiB sparse $A$ pays 14--15 cycles (Fig.~\ref{fig:delay_a}(b)). It recurs on every instruction, because the $A$ buffer refills at each instruction's first compute micro-op: a descriptor names an address, and the kernel may rewrite the tile under it. The H100 shows all three signatures: dense SS mode completes 9.0--10.0 cycles after RS mode from $N{=}16$ to 128, sparse pays alike (8.0--9.0) because it also reads 2\,KiB of $A$, and the delay stays at 10.25 cycles when every instruction reads the same $A$. The H100's warp-group MMA thus behaves as one that stages $A$ from shared memory before its first multiplication and keeps no copy between instructions, as FourShadow does.

\answerbox{Answer \#1 for Q1}{A warp-group MMA in SS mode waits for $A$ before its first multiplication, a fixed delay per instruction that does not grow with the tile width; the H100 and FourShadow keep no operand between instructions, so every instruction pays the delay again.}

\subsection{Sparse Tensor Core Design Space Exploration}\label{sec:eval_sparse}

This subsection continues Q1 with the internal mechanisms behind tensor-core sparsity (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). The first part explores the symmetric and asymmetric layouts of warp-level MMA on FourShadow. The next two give the answers by comparing FourShadow with NVIDIA GPUs: warp-level MMA with 2:4 sparsity with an RTX 5080 and an H100, and warp-group MMA with an H100; the last measures the metadata load on both GPUs (Sections~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_halves}, \ref{sec:eval_sparse_wg} and~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_meta}, configuration in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\subsubsection{Symmetric vs.\ Asymmetric Sparse Configurations}\label{sec:eval_sparse_sym}
Sparsity speeds up the asymmetric layouts in every cell of
Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_sym_sweep} and the symmetric layouts less. The 8- and
32-thread cells span 1.24--1.78$\times$, with the maximum for TF32 at
$K{=}1024$ and 8 threads, and both symmetric cells sit below both
asymmetric cells in 11 of 12 rows (the exception: TF32 at $K{=}256$,
1.45$\times$ at 16 threads against 1.37$\times$ at 8). At 4 threads
sparsity loses in nine of 12 cells (0.71--0.83$\times$); at 16 threads
it gains modestly, 0.91--1.45$\times$ and above 1 in 11 of 12 cells. The
ordering follows Section~\ref{sec:design_sparse}: only the asymmetric
layouts halve the micro-ops of a sparse WMMA. The symmetric layouts keep
the dense micro-op count, which therefore explains neither the 4-thread
loss nor the 16-thread gain; their source is not isolated.

\subsubsection{Only Part of the Sparse Cost Halves}\label{sec:eval_sparse_halves}

For the $64{\times}64{\times}K$ FP16 GEMM of Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(a), the dense-over-sparse speedup rises with $K$ but stays below 2$\times$: from 1.16$\times$ at $K{=}32$ to 1.50$\times$ at $K{=}512$ on the RTX 5080, and from 1.07$\times$ to 1.56$\times$ on FourShadow at 32 threads per warp. Over issue widths of 1, 2 and 4 with 4 or 16 warps, at 8 and 32 threads, FourShadow stays below 2$\times$: its maximum is 1.92$\times$, for the INT8 $128{\times}128{\times}1024$ GEMM at (IW-1, NW-16) and 32 threads.

We fit the cycles of each $K$ sweep as $C_{\mathrm{dense}} = c + (a+d)K$ and $C_{\mathrm{sparse}} = c' + (a/2+d)K$, where $a$ is the per-$K$ cost that 2:4 sparsity is assumed to halve exactly, and $d$ the per-$K$ cost that it leaves unchanged ($c$ and $c'$ do not depend on $K$). The fitted slopes $s_{\mathrm{dense}} = a+d$ and $s_{\mathrm{sparse}} = a/2+d$ give the halvable share $f = a/(a+d) = 2(1 - s_{\mathrm{sparse}}/s_{\mathrm{dense}})$, and as $K$ grows the speedup approaches the bound $1/(1-f/2) = s_{\mathrm{dense}}/s_{\mathrm{sparse}}$. For FP16, $f$ is 69.7\% on the RTX 5080 and 78.5\% on FourShadow at 32 threads, which gives the dashed lines of Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(a) at 1.53$\times$ and 1.65$\times$. Here $a$ contains the tensor-core math and the $A$ load, which halve together, and $d$ the $B$ load and the metadata. At 8 threads per warp FourShadow's instruction counts confirm this split: the halved $A$-fragment loads and WMMA micro-ops predict $a$ within 2.2\%.

Sparsity is faster in each of the 17 shapes measured on both FourShadow and the RTX 5080, and the two speedups differ by a median of 6.2\% at 32 threads and 9.0\% at 8 threads. Only the FP16 fits are valid on the RTX 5080: the TF32 fit has a negative dense intercept, $-$50,243 cycles, and the INT8 fit has only four values of $K$, so Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(b) shows FP16 alone. On an H100 the same $64{\times}64{\times}K$ FP16 GEMM gives a halvable share of 54.4\% and a bound of 1.37$\times$, and FourShadow at 8 threads per warp gives 50.7\% and 1.34$\times$. The share therefore differs between GPU generations and between FourShadow configurations, while the cost structure, a per-$K$ cost of which sparsity halves only a part, holds on all of them. On the NVIDIA GPUs this reading of $a$ and $d$ is an interpretation: no counter at the in-kernel region separates them. For warp-group MMA on the H100 the data type does not matter: TF32, FP16, BF16 and E4M3 give the same issue intervals and latencies within 0.042 cycles.

\answerbox{Answer \#2 for Q1}{2:4 sparsity halves only part of the per-$K$ cost of a warp-level tensor-core GEMM, so its speedup stays below 2$\times$ and depends on the size of that halvable part: for FP16 it is 69.7\% on the RTX 5080 (bound 1.53$\times$) and 54.4\% on the H100 (1.37$\times$).}

\subsubsection{The Halvable Share Falls as the Warp-Group Tile Widens}\label{sec:eval_sparse_wg}

With identical staging in the dense and sparse kernels, 2:4 sparsity speeds up FourShadow's warp-group GEMM by 1.41--1.53$\times$ and the H100's by 1.28--1.51$\times$, below 2$\times$ at every $K$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_wg_halvable}(a)). FourShadow runs the $128{\times}128{\times}K$ GEMM at (IW-4, NW-4), 32 threads per warp and $\mathit{NRC}{=}8$, timed in total kernel cycles, with the metadata loaded once before the loop and the warps copying $A$ and $B$ into shared memory before each warp-group MMA (synchronous staging); its dense and sparse kernels differ only in the length of $A$ per $K$ step and in the MMA instruction. The H100 runs one warpgroup's $64{\times}16{\times}K$ loop, the tile of equal $N$ (Section~\ref{sec:method_hw}) and pipelines its operand staging, overlapping it with the warp-group MMA. Both speedups rise with $K$ toward their bounds.

The halvable-share model of Section~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_halves} fits these sweeps as well, with positive intercepts and seven (H100) or eight (FourShadow) values of $K$; at $N{=}16$ its bounds are the dashed lines of Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_wg_halvable}(a), 1.44--1.54$\times$.

On both machines $f$ falls as the tile widens (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_wg_halvable}(b)): in the H100's pipelined loop from 69.2 to 58.2\% in RS mode and from 61.2 to 36.2\% in SS mode between $N{=}16$ and 64, and on FourShadow with synchronous staging from 68.4 to 56.4\% and from 70.2 to 54.8\% between $N{=}16$ and 32. Sparsity halves $A$ but not $B$, and $B$ grows with $N$ while $A$ does not. On the H100 this follows from the operand sizes: per $K$, a sparse instruction halves the math and the bytes of the 64-row $A$ but carries the same bytes of the $N$-column $B$. On FourShadow the instruction counts show it: sparsity halves the $A$ copy and the micro-ops of the warp-group MMA, while the $B$ copy is the same in both kernels and doubles from $\mathit{NRC}{=}8$ to 16; in SS mode at 32 threads these two halved parts account for the fitted $a$ within 1\%.

The same parts halve in RS and SS mode on both machines, the math and $A$ but not $B$, yet in this loop the H100 gains more in RS than in SS mode, by 8 to 22 points of $f$, while FourShadow gains about equally in both, within 2 points. On FourShadow the share also depends on how exposed the staging of $A$ is: at $N{=}16$ it is 68--70\% with synchronous staging and 27--31\% with double-buffered DXA staging, which hides the transfer of $A$ so that only the micro-ops of the warp-group MMA halve; the two machines therefore agree in how $f$ falls with $N$, not in its size.

\answerbox{Answer \#3 for Q1}{2:4 sparsity halves the $A$ operand of a warp-group MMA GEMM but not $B$, which grows with the tile width $N$, so the halvable share falls as the tile widens, on both the H100 (pipelined loop) and FourShadow.}

\subsubsection{An Early Metadata Load Hides Behind Independent Work}\label{sec:eval_sparse_meta}
2:4 sparsity needs metadata that marks the kept elements of $A$. On NVIDIA GPUs the sparse MMA takes it as an operand (\texttt{mma.sp}~\cite{nvidia2026ptxisa}), and \texttt{HMMA.SP} in our compiled kernels reads it from a register, so a kernel loads the metadata before the MMA, an overhead that sparsity adds. We measure this overhead in a loop of a sparse FP16 MMA and $n\in\{0,4,16,64\}$ dependent integer filler operations, independent of the MMA and its metadata. The metadata load sits in one of three places, as the compiled code confirms: once before the loop (resident), before the filler (early) or directly before the MMA (late). A reload adds the early or late minus the resident cycles per iteration at equal $n$, so the late schedule exposes its full cost.

On the RTX 5080 (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse_nvidia}(c)), one warp's late reload adds 363.1 to 364.2 cycles at every $n$, and its early reload 326.2 cycles at $n{=}0$ and 0.3 at $n{=}64$. The warp issues the filler while the load is pending, so an early reload costs the larger of the load and the filler and a late one their sum; both rules hold within 0.13\% for FP16 and 0.72\% over six data formats. An H100 follows them within 0.45\%: one warp's late reload adds 293.9 to 295.1 cycles, and its early reload falls from 246.1 cycles at $n{=}0$ to 2.1 at $n{=}64$. Independent work that outlasts the load therefore hides an early reload. FourShadow at (IW-1, NW-4) shows the same hiding when four one-warp CTAs share the issue lane at 32 threads per warp: the early reload's added cycles fall from 28.7 per CTA at $n{=}4$ to 7.6 at $n{=}64$, while the late reload's stay at 58.0 to 62.1.

\subsection{FPGA Design Flow}\label{sec:eval_fpga}

We implement FourShadow on an AMD Alveo U55C with 16\,GB HBM2 and
target a 250\,MHz clock frequency. The evaluated system contains
one core with eight 8-thread warps, an issue width of four, and four
8-lane Ten-Four\cite{rout2026tenfouropensourcefuseddot} blocks supporting FP16 inputs and FP32
accumulation, WGMMA, 2K-wide FEDP, and 2:4 structured sparsity. Table.~\ref{tab:tcu-parts} shows the hierarchical utilization overhead of each FourShadow extended unit.
Of the post-synthesis LUT area of the fully enabled Vortex core, the TCU
takes 53.7\%, the issue stage and front end 17.2\%, the FPU 13.8\%, the ALU
6.1\%, memory (first-level caches and local memory) 4.8\%, the LSU 2.1\%,
the SFU 1.6\% and the remainder 0.7\%.

Fig.~\ref{fig:e2e_fpga} shows that WGMMA provides its largest gains
when matrix operations dominate execution. It raises NeRF from
4.72$\times$ to 26.17$\times$, Llama~2 prefill from 5.91$\times$ to
9.15$\times$, and decode from 1.42$\times$ to 2.00$\times$, while CNN,
attention, and FlashAttention remain nearly unchanged. Sparsity alone
benefits NeRF and slightly improves attention, but its metadata and
compression overhead reduce performance for CNN and both Llama~2
phases. Combining sparsity with WGMMA reaches 16.88$\times$ for CNN
and 26.82$\times$ for NeRF, showing that the two extensions are
workload-dependent rather than uniformly additive.

\subsection{ASIC Design Flow}\label{sec:eval_asic}

\subsubsection{ASIC Synthesis and Timing}

We synthesize the standalone FourShadow TCU using Synopsys Design
Compiler X-2025.06-SP1 with the ASAP7 7\,nm
\texttt{asap7sc7p5t\_28} NLDM TT predictive PDK library.

\subsubsection{Threads/Warp Area--Performance Tradeoff}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} combines synthesized TCU area with end-to-end application evaluation performance. Llama~2 prefill reaches 12.7$\times$ at $NT{=}16$
and then slightly regresses at $NT{=}32$, despite the TCU area more
than doubling. Decode shows a stronger optimum, rising to
6.2$\times$ at $NT{=}16$ before falling to approximately 3$\times$
at $NT{=}32$. NeRF instead continues to benefit from wider warps and
reaches its highest speedup at $NT{=}32$. The preferred warp width is
therefore workload-dependent, although $NT{=}16$ consistently provides the best
area--performance ratio for both Llama~2 phases.

\subsubsection{Issue-Width Area--Performance Tradeoff}
Fig.~\ref{fig:iw_area_scaling} shows that TCU area scales almost
linearly with issue width, increasing from 58.6\,k$\mu$m$^2$ at
$IW{=}1$ to 115.6 and 230.6\,k$\mu$m$^2$ at $IW{=}2$ and $IW{=}4$.
However, performance scales much more slowly, reaching 1.37--1.41$\times$ at
$IW{=}2$ and 1.67--1.71$\times$ at $IW{=}4$. Doubling the area again
to an estimated 455--462\,k$\mu$m$^2$ at $IW{=}8$ raises speedup to
only 1.70--1.81$\times$. Thus, $IW{=}4$ forms the practical optimal on the curve, beyond which additional TCU blocks and larger warp-groups are increasingly
limited by the core's instruction front end (Section~\ref{sec:eval_iw}) rather than
arithmetic capacity.
