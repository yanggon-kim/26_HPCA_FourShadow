\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Tensor Cores~\cite{nvidia2017volta, nvidia2020ampere, nvidia2022hopper, nvidia2024blackwell} and Matrix Cores~\cite{amd2020cdna1, amd2021cdna2, amd2023cdna3, amd2025cdna4} carry the AI throughput of modern GPUs: they execute matrix multiply-accumulate (MMA) operations at far higher throughput than the SIMT pipelines beside them. Unlike the systolic arrays of standalone accelerators such as Google's TPUs~\cite{jouppi2017indatacenterperformanceanalysistensor, jouppi2020domain, norrie2021design, jouppi2021ten, jouppi2023tpuv4} and AWS Trainium~\cite{aws_trainium_architecture}, a GPU tensor core is a functional unit of the SIMT core. Each generation has raised its throughput with larger MMA tiles, MMA instructions shared by the four warps of a warpgroup, operands read from shared memory~\cite{luo2025dissectingnvidiahopperarchitecture}, and 2:4 structured sparsity~\cite{nvidia2020ampere, mishra2021acceleratingsparsedeepneural}.

This throughput has been bought with silicon and power. Dennard scaling, which kept the power per mm$^2$ of silicon nearly constant as transistors shrank, has ended~\cite{hennessy2019golden}, and the energy efficiency of transistors no longer keeps pace with their number~\cite{esmaeilzadeh2011dark}. From the V100 to the B200, NVIDIA's dense FP16 tensor throughput grew from 125 to 2,200 TFLOPS, while the transistor count grew from 21.1 to 208 billion and the board power from 300 to 1,000\,W~\cite{nvidia2017volta, nvidia2024blackwell}. The die stayed at about 820\,mm$^2$ through the A100 and the H100~\cite{nvidia2020ampere, nvidia2022hopper}, and the B200 joins two reticle-limited dies~\cite{nvidia2024blackwell}. AMD's Instinct GPUs follow the same trend, with the maximum power rising from 560\,W for the MI250X to 1,400\,W for the MI355X~\cite{amd2021cdna2, amd2025cdna4}. Throughput per watt has therefore grown far more slowly than throughput, and power depends on how the matrix units are organized, not only on how many there are: in RTL, integrating them at the cluster instead of the core cuts on-chip active power by tens of percent~\cite{kim2025virgo}. Whether a tensor-core feature or design point is worth its silicon and power is thus a question of efficiency.

Judging this efficiency requires answers to two questions, which organize this paper. \textbf{Q1: What is the microarchitectural behavior behind NVIDIA's tensor-core features?} Vendor documents and microbenchmarks report what each feature delivers, not the mechanism that produces it: NVIDIA states that 2:4 sparsity doubles tensor-core throughput~\cite{nvidia2020ampere}, yet the GEMM speedup approaches 2$\times$ only for larger GEMMs~\cite{mishra2021acceleratingsparsedeepneural}, and sparse \texttt{mma} instructions on an H800 average 1.42$\times$ the dense throughput~\cite{luo2025dissectingnvidiahopperarchitecture}. \textbf{Q2: How does a tensor core behave as its architectural parameters change?} A commercial GPU ships one point of the design space, with its threads per warp and issue width fixed in silicon; the design points around it are unmeasured, and so is whether that point is the best one.

Current platforms answer neither question (Table~\ref{tab:related_work_comparison}). Microbenchmarking studies of NVIDIA Tensor Cores~\cite{jia2018dissectingnvidiavoltagpu, 8695642, markidis2018tensorcore, martineau2019v100, jia2019dissectingnvidiaturingt4, yan2020demystifying, 9926299, 9931992, 10579250, luo2025dissectingnvidiahopperarchitecture, 10.1145/3725843.3756041, 11575375} and AMD Matrix Cores~\cite{10590025, kurzynski2025mfma, jarmusch2026microbenchmark} measure shipped silicon, so they cannot change the organization behind an outcome, and NVIDIA's documents give the area and power of the whole GPU only~\cite{nvidia2017volta, nvidia2020ampere, nvidia2022hopper, nvidia2024blackwell}. Simulators abstract the tensor core: Accel-Sim~\cite{accel-sim} models a tensor-core instruction by a latency and an initiation interval, GPGPU-Sim~\cite{raihan2019modeling} has no sparsity, and both fix the warp at 32 threads and have no RTL. Research platforms each lack a piece: Virgo~\cite{kim2025virgo} has no sparsity, CoopWarp~\cite{nada2025cooperative} neither sparsity nor warp-group MMA, and the sparse tensor cores RM-STC~\cite{huang2023rmstc}, VEGETA~\cite{jeong2023vegeta} and Sparse TC~\cite{zhu2019sparsetensorcore} no warp-group MMA and no open source. No prior platform combines configurable threads per warp and issue width, warp-group MMA, 2:4 sparsity, seven data types and open, synthesizable RTL; the others support one to four data types each.

To this end, this paper presents \textit{\textbf{FourShadow}}, the white-box counterpart of a commercial tensor core, built on the open-source RISC-V Vortex GPGPU~\cite{vortex}: it implements the same features with internals that can be read and changed (Figure~\ref{fig:figure_integration}). FourShadow is an open, synthesizable and reconfigurable tensor-core microarchitecture that obtains the causes by design-space exploration, changing one microarchitectural parameter at a time. The name has two meanings. As a \emph{shadow}, it outlines an object that cannot be inspected, in four components: the base tensor core, the warp-group tensor core, the sparse tensor core and the programming model. We measure it beside an RTX 5080 and an H100 to answer Q1: where the two agree, the mechanism visible in FourShadow is a candidate explanation; where they differ, the difference excludes one. As a \emph{foreshadow}, being reconfigurable, it foretells the trends of design points that no commercial GPU ships, which answers Q2; its best point carries over to a commercial design only as far as the two match. Threads per warp, issue width, accumulator registers, operand staging, dot-product length and data format are build parameters. Performance is measured in cycles on SimX and end to end on an FPGA, and area by ASIC synthesis in ASAP7.

Building these features on RISC-V is not a port of NVIDIA's design, because the RISC-V instruction set sets the limits. RV32 gives a thread 32 floating-point registers, so $A$, $B$ and the accumulator get eight each, and the tile shape follows this register budget and the threads per warp. A tensor-core micro-op inherits the four register fields of the R4-type \texttt{fmadd} encoding, so the doubled $B$ operand of a sparse micro-op fits in one register only in the configurations whose $B$ block fills half a register. Warp-group MMA needs operands named by shared-memory descriptors, a tile buffer whose $B$ buffer all warps share, and consistency checks in place of a barrier, because the warps of a warpgroup advance independently. The 2:4 metadata needs its own load instruction and, in the RTL, a scoreboard bit that orders the sparse MMA after it. Section~\ref{sec:design} presents these solutions.

FourShadow answers both questions with measurements that no shipped GPU or simulator can produce. For Q2, per-lane warp-group MMA throughput peaks at 16 threads per warp, and NVIDIA's 32 threads sit on that plateau (Section~\ref{sec:eval_threads}).

A wider warpgroup scales the warp-group MMA but not the kernel around it, because the core fetches one warp instruction per cycle: with the warps grown to match, an issue width of 8 speeds up a $K$ step by 3.6--3.9$\times$, not 8$\times$ (Section~\ref{sec:eval_iw}). For warp-level MMA, sparsity speeds up the asymmetric configurations by 1.24--1.78$\times$ and the symmetric ones less (Section~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_sym}). For Q1, 2:4 sparsity halves only part of the per-$K$ cost of a GEMM, so its speedup stays below 2$\times$ (Section~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_halves}), and for warp-group MMA that part shrinks as the tile widens, on FourShadow and an H100 alike (Section~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_wg}). A shipped GPU fixes its threads per warp, issue width and tile path and gives no area of its tensor cores, and a simulator has no tensor-core organization to vary and no RTL to synthesize; neither question can be approached without an open, reconfigurable design.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We build FourShadow, an open-source, synthesizable tensor-core framework on the RISC-V Vortex GPGPU, with warp-group MMA in register/shared-memory (RS) and shared-memory/shared-memory (SS) operand staging and a 2:4 structured-sparsity datapath (Section~\ref{sec:design}).

    \item We answer Q2 by sweeping threads per warp and issue width: per-lane throughput peaks at 16 threads, and the core's instruction front end limits a wider warpgroup (Section~\ref{sec:eval_hierarchy}).

    \item We answer Q1 by measuring FourShadow beside an RTX 5080 and an H100: the share of the per-$K$ cost that sparsity halves bounds the sparse speedup, and it falls as the warp-group tile widens (Section~\ref{sec:eval_sparse}).

    \item We report RISC-V design findings: the sparse speedup depends on symmetric versus asymmetric configurations, a 2K-wide FEDP outweighs its deeper pipeline, and WGMMA descriptors belong in the register file rather than TCU SRAM (Sections~\ref{sec:eval_wgmma} and~\ref{sec:eval_sparse_sym}).

    \item We validate FourShadow by FPGA implementation and ASIC synthesis. On an AMD Alveo U55C FPGA at 250\,MHz, WGMMA raises the end-to-end speedup over the SIMT baseline from $5.91\times$ to $9.15\times$ for Llama~2 prefill and from $1.42\times$ to $2.00\times$ for decode, and the combined extensions reach $16.88\times$ on CNN and $26.82\times$ on NeRF workloads.
\end{itemize}
